General XRD optics

Soller slits

A stack of thin parallel plates of an X-ray-absorbing metal (for example molybdenum or stainless steel; the material varies between makers) that limits axial divergence, how steeply rays leave the scattering plane. It can sit on the incident side, the diffracted side or both.

Incident Soller slit and axial divergence

Each channel passes rays up to a limiting axial angle α/2, where the full acceptance angle α = 2 arctan(g/ℓ) ≈ 2g/ℓ for plate gap g and plate length ℓ; steeper rays hit a plate and are absorbed. Transmitted rays keep their direction. Angles are exaggerated.

A tighter Soller slit accepts fewer inclined rays, so the low-angle tail shrinks and the intensity falls. The Bragg angle does not change: axial divergence spreads intensity to lower angles, so the apparent maximum, and more so the centroid, sits slightly below the true 2θ. An asymmetric profile fit recovers the true position. The curves come from a simple model in which a ray tilted by ψ out of the scattering plane (up to α/2, the limit drawn on the left) is received ∝ ψ² lower in 2θ and show the trend only, not measured profiles; the available acceptances depend on the instrument.

Why axial divergence makes low-angle peaks asymmetric

Diffraction cones meet the straight receiving slit as curved arcs. A ray inclined by ψ out of the scattering plane arrives at a slightly lower 2θ, by about Δ(2θ) ≈ (ψ²/2) cot 2θ below 90°.

  • The shift is largest at low 2θ, where the rings are most strongly curved, giving a tail on the low-angle side of the peak.
  • It vanishes near 2θ = 90° and changes sign above 90°, where the tail moves to the high-angle side.
  • A Soller slit does not shorten the receiving slit. It removes rays inclined by more than about half its acceptance angle, so ψ, and therefore the tail, is reduced at the cost of intensity. Incident- and diffracted-side Soller slits limit the inclination of the incident and diffracted rays respectively; both contribute.
